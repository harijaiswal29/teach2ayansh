          For grown-ups

          How each stop follows the book

          1. Moonlight Reading (pages 7–8): the whole poem, a few verses at a time. “Read to me” reads it aloud and lights up each line. The four book words (hover, dales, dainty, horrid) and ten more hard words can be tapped for a meaning and a real-life example. One simple question follows each part.
          2. Rhyme Stars (page 8): every verse rhymes on lines 2 and 4 (brown/down, bed/head, fish/wish…). He catches the rhyming word and then sees the two lines together.
          3. Magic Wand (page 9, question a): why she waves her wand, which way she waves it for a good child (right to left) and a bad child (left to right), and what she looks like.
          4. Two Dream Worlds (page 9, question b): he sorts 15 things from the poem into the pleasant and the horrid dream, and the chart from the book fills in.
          5. Dropped Dream Cards (pages 9–10, question c): tick only the cards that are really in the poem. Round 1 uses the six cards from the book.
          6. Real or Make-believe (page 10, question d): to explain “imaginative”, he first sorts things into real and make-believe. Then he answers the book question. Talking birds and the fairy fish fountain are both accepted, because both are make-believe.
          7. Dream Ladder (page 10, question e): put the events in order, climbing from the bottom rung, like the ladder in the book.
          8. Pronoun Pals (page 11, question f): swap a naming word for he, she, it or they, then build the book's example sentences with she, it and they.
          9. Royal Ball (pages 13–14): match masculine and feminine pairs. It uses every pair from both pages, plus lion/lioness and tiger/tigress from the poem.
          10. Royal Guestbook (page 15): fill in the guestbook from the word bank. Each line has a clue word (“his” or “her”), so he learns to look for it. Then he changes whole sentences from masculine to feminine.
          11. Question Quest (page 16): full stop or question mark, capital letters (including “I”), and building a question about each of the three pictures.
          12. Sleepy Teddy (pages 11–12): the Real Route bedtime habits, a bedtime routine to put in order, and the Heritage Route “Then and Now”.

          Notes on the answers

          • Question e: the ladder in the book is read from the bottom up, so the order is: the child falls asleep, Queen Mab comes from the moon, she waves her wand, the child dreams. If he says Queen Mab arrives first, that is a sensible reading too. Talk about it.
          • Question d has no single answer in the book. Ask him why he picked his image. That “why” is the real point of the question.
          • The poem calls the child “it”, which is old-fashioned English. In Pronoun Pals, “it” is used only for things and animals.
          • Verse 7 is scary (ogres with knives). The game keeps it short and reminds him that it is only a dream.
          • Question f also asks him to write his own sentences. After Pronoun Pals, ask him to say one sentence each with she, it and they, then write them in the book.

          Do it for real

          • Read the poem at bedtime. Let him be Queen Mab with a pencil “wand”: right to left for good dreams, left to right for horrid ones.
          • At a farm or the zoo, play “find the pair”: hen and rooster, cow and bull, lion and lioness.
          • At dinner, each person asks one question. Everyone listens for the question word at the start (what, where, can, why).
          • Glow-worms are fireflies. Thousands light up in the Sahyadri hills of Maharashtra just before the monsoon, which makes a lovely real-life link to the poem.
