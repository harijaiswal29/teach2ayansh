            For grown-ups

            This follows English Chapter 1, “The Rainbow”, in Ayansh's book (pages 7–32). Each stop uses the book's own poem, words and exercises, in the same order. Every new idea starts with a short “💡” card that explains it with an example before any question. Teaching is read aloud by default (switch it off at the top). The last round of each stop is an on your own round: no clue until his second try. A wrong answer costs a little, so three stars means very few slips.

            Part 1 · The poem (pages 7–10)

            1. Where Do Clouds Go? (page 7): the opening question, answered his own way, then what pushes clouds (the wind). Extra, not in the book: how sun and rain make a rainbow, and the seven colours in order.
            2. Read the Poem (page 8): the whole poem by Christina Rossetti, read aloud line by line as he follows. The five word helpers from the page (sail, prettier, heaven, bridges, overtops) as flip cards, a meaning check, then “which picture fits this line?”.
            3. What the Poem Means (pages 9–10): About the Poet (born December 5, 1830, died December 29, 1894; poet, storyteller, nature lover, dreamer) and her two personal questions. He reads each line aloud himself (question 1–2). Then question 3: a. on the rivers; c. goes higher than the trees; d. the rainbow looks like a curved road from the ground up to the sky; e. a new title (his choice). “The bow” is taught as the rainbow, curved like a bow.
            4. Rhyme Time (page 10, question 3b): seas–these and please–trees from the poem, then rhyme picks, odd one out, and finish-the-rhyme.

            Part 2 · Out and about (pages 11–13)

            1. Nature Detective Walk (pages 11–12): I spy, I hear, I touch, and I wonder questions with a park picture. Please also do the real walk and fill in the pages.
            2. Trash to Treasure Trail (page 13, Eco Route): the three words Recycled, Reused and Refused with the book's meanings, then sorting everyday things.

            Part 3 · Nouns (pages 13–27)

            1. Common and Proper Nouns (pages 13–15): person, place, animal or thing; the book's examples sorted; the circus poster; page 14 question 2 (monkey, Ananya, Hanging garden, uncle, Zara); and two sentences built from page 15's nouns, with the capital letter.
            2. Plural Patrol (pages 15–17): one or more than one (astronaut, quails, fairies, volcano); add s or es (s, ss, sh, ch, x); y to ies, -o words and special plurals; the Detour mistakes (mans, babys, boxs, cat's, mouses); then he spells plurals himself.
            3. Snack Tray, Basket and Zoo (pages 18–21): plurals of the snack tray, the laundry basket table (t-shirts, box, brushes, socks, baskets, teddy bears, towel) and the national park (1 bear, giraffes, no second monkey, and the singular sentence “The child saw an elephant and a giraffe at the zoo.”).
            4. Count It or Not? (pages 21–23): countable and uncountable, numbers versus some, much, a little, the kitchen picture, and a shopping list with 4 of each.
            5. Five-Senses Nouns (pages 24–25): concrete nouns; bell–hear, flower–smell, and pencil, apple and dress with see or touch; then four things for his school bag.
            6. Collective Nouns (pages 25–27): flock, herd, pack, swarm, class, crew, family, bunch, pile, bouquet, parliament of owls, school of fish; the five underline sentences; and the book's word search (FLOCK, HERD, PACK, SWARM, TEAM, SCHOOL).

            Part 4 · Sounds, story and writing (pages 27–32)

            1. Trigraph Tongue Twisters (pages 27–28): the ten trigraphs (igh, tch, dge, sch, shr, spl, spr, str, thr, squ), the tongue twisters, and the Secret Trigraph Code: splash, spring, squid, shrub, square.
            2. Double Letter Twins (page 28): rabbit, bell, happy, ladder, egg, then the circle-the-words list (apple, glass, running, hopping, pillow, bell, dress, hill).
            3. Max's Amazing Race (pages 29–30): the listening story with its ace words (place, race, face, space) and ame words (name, game, flame, frame). The story is retold in my own words, with the same events and the same eight words.
            4. If I Could Touch a Rainbow (pages 30–32): he picks an ending for each of the five sentences, then builds the whole sentence word by word, starting with a capital letter and ending with a full stop. His five sentences are shown at the end to copy onto page 32.
            5. Grand Review: 10 mixed questions from all four parts, new each time, and a Chapter 1 Word Wizard certificate that shows which part to revisit.

            Notes and small choices

            • Open questions (where the clouds go, a new title, art lover, I wonder, favourite find, the rainbow writing) accept any answer. For the silly options in the writing (eat it like a candy, wet socks, more homework…) the game smiles and accepts them, as the book asks for “the best” in his view.
            • Page 24 has two “touch” labels, one “see”, one “smell” and one “hear”. The game accepts see or touch for the pencil, the apple and the dress, as long as the five labels fit.
            • On the circus poster I counted “The Grand Arena”, “April” and “Titan” as proper nouns, and circus, strongman and flyers as common nouns.
            • The book's tongue twisters are for saying fast, not trigraph words, except “Catch the kitten in the kitchen” (tch).

            Do it for real

            • Read the poem together, then let him read it to you or in the mirror (page 10).
            • Go on the Nature Detective Walk and the Trash to Treasure Trail, and sort what you find.
            • Write a real shopping list with him: 4 countable and 4 uncountable things.
            • On a sunny day, stand with your back to the sun and spray water from a hose or a spray bottle to make your own rainbow.
